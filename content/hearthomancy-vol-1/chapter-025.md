<p>“I don’t see why we’ve got to clean out the basement,” Lorelei grumbled.</p>

<p>“Because some crazy Scholar is willing to pay us ten thousand dollars for a visit,” I answered evenly. “She asked for a basement room to be cleared out <em>for her accommodations</em>.”</p>

<p>In all honesty, until Ayara had read the part of the letter about paying me, I was tempted to tell the Scholar to shove it, Skill Book or no Skill Book. But ten thousand dollars was a lot of money.</p>

<p>I had done a handful of renovation work and handyman jobs, but not enough to fix the dent I had put in my bank account by finishing the servant’s quarters. Randy was still buying everything I could bottle, but if I wanted any kind of buffer after making my next mortgage payment, I’d happily check the Scholar’s check.</p>

<p>Besides, I had to admit that I was curious to meet another Drow. Ayara was a knockout, but I had to assume her strength and stature set her apart from her friends and peers. <em>What would Ishikana look like?</em></p>

<p>A week had passed since Ishikana had sent her letter. Apparently, she had business in New York City before she could visit. That gave us time to fix up the house further and clean out the basement.</p>

<p>It was Saturday, and Ayara was determined to spend the weekend at her forge. She was working on my new weapon. She had convinced me to eschew a wand and instead go for a sword that would act as my magical focus.</p>

<p>“Spellswords are a common Class amongst the Drow,” the Blacksmith had explained. “It’s a Class offered to Wizards who practice martial Proficiencies. Many Masters of the Sacred Flames use it as their secondary Class.”</p>

<p>She was convinced a Drow rapier would be the perfect weapon for me.</p>

<p>I didn’t know shit about sword fighting, but I figured if I became enamored with the weapon, I could pick up Weapon Master’s Boon at some point. I just wish I knew how to finish the Lair Quest we were on. Setting up the enchanted gate and the stakes hadn’t been enough to meet its requirements.</p>

<p>Shaking my head, I tried to focus on cleaning out the basement. I made a mental note to come up with some excuse if Merle came around again since what we were doing could one hundred percent be considered renting out a room in a half-demolished structure. There was probably something buried in the paperwork that frowned upon such practices, and I did not want my permits pulled.</p>

<p>I had decided to clear out the wine cellar for Ishikana’s room. I wasn’t sure if she had some kind of sunlight sensitivity. Ayara seemed perfectly fine working outdoors, but maybe her friend preferred the dark.</p>

<p>The wine cellar was large, with racks upon racks running in uneven aisles. Most of them were empty, but a few still held dusty bottles.</p>

<p>As I pulled them off the shelves, I hit them with my new Identify Skill.</p>

<p><b>Identify</b><br><b>MP Cost: </b>50</p>

<p><em>Allows the user to pull System-relevant data about a person or an object. High levels of this Skill allow for more detailed information.</em></p>

<p>“Junk,” I confirmed as nothing came up.</p>

<p>Identify only worked on people or items touched by the System. Using it on a mundane object gave you nothing back.</p>

<p>As far as I could tell, it overlapped with a lot of other perception Skills, like Ayara’s Eyes of Discernment. The Skill was traditionally very slow to level up, but as you did, you could tease more information out of the System.</p>

<p>According to the Blacksmith, people often fused Identify with other, more specialized Skills, or evolved it once they reached a higher level.</p>

<p>Ishikana had given me the Skill Book because she was dissatisfied with my observations thus far. She saw giving me the Skill Book as an investment on her part, a way to pull more insights from Rotwood Manor.</p>

<p>If nothing else, it might prove handy if we found another relic, like Ayara’s anvil. I had no idea what we would do, though, if we found a bottle of magical wine.</p>

<p>“Junk.” I tossed another bottle of vinegar into the trash can.</p>

<p>“Hey, Brent?” Lorelei called. “I think I found something!”</p>

<p>The Hearth Spirit had drifted out of the room, moving down a side passage I hadn’t even noticed before. Following my bond with her, I found her in a storage room. The air was stuffy and smelled of dust.</p>

<p>Mana swirled heavily around us. The room was jammed packed with old chests and steamer trunks. The few that were propped open were full of moth-eaten clothes. I could also see various paw prints in the dust, indicating Stinky had chased out a few mice.</p>

<p>“We’ve got to give that cat a raise,” I noted.</p>

<p>Lorelei was floating over an old steamer trunk. The leather was rotted, and the wood was chipped and splintered. A brass lock held it shut.</p>

<p>Mana hung heavily around the trunk. Darkness, Life, and Water seeped out from the lid.</p>

<p>“What’s inside?” I asked the Hearth Spirit.</p>

<p>She shook her head. “No idea, sorry. I was just cleaning when I felt it—call out to me.”</p>

<p>I tried to get the lid open, but the lock was stuck tight. Despite its decrepit state, the chest refused to open.</p>

<p>With no other options, I hauled the trunk back upstairs and into the main hall. Ayara was still hard at work, I could feel the mana rippling away from her anvil with each hammer strike, so I decided to leave her to her forge.</p>

<p>Grabbing a crowbar, I tried to leverage the box open, but the chest held fast. Reaching into the house’s mana, I pulled on a bit of Fire mana to enhance my strength.</p>

<p>The lid was ripped off its hinges with my next effort. The crowbar was almost sent flying out of my hands.</p>

<p>Covering my mouth, I coughed as a cloud of dust filled the air. Lorelei churned a current of Wind mana and blew it out a nearby window.</p>

<p>Fanning away the last of the dust, I glanced inside the box. It was thickly packed with old newspaper clippings and journals.</p>

<p>Most of the newspapers were so faded and brittle as to be illegible. I could only make out the faintest traces of the newsprint, indicating they had been printed sometime in the late 1800s.</p>

<p>“They’re even older than the house,” Lorelei said as she hovered over my shoulder. “Did Lorelei’s father collect them or something?”</p>

<p>The newspaper and journals crumbled as soon as I tried to touch them.</p>

<p>In the corner of the steamer trunk, there was a small, wooden box. Like the journals, it fell apart as soon as I tried to pick it up. Inside, I found an old mason jar. To my surprise, water sloshed inside.</p>

<p><b>Waters of Styx (Unknown Grade)</b></p>

<p><em>Waters from the river that bound the Underworld. Bound by the Oath of the Gods, this water represents the sanctity of promises made and the price of bargains betrayed. </em></p>

<p><em>“With this, I bind you, and I damn you for all eternity.”—Eustace Phelps</em></p>

<p class="scene-break">***</p>

<p>The jar sat in the middle of our dinner table. It didn’t look like much, but I noticed when light passed through the water, instead of forming a rainbow, it broke up light into a gray, distorted pattern, like TV static.</p>

<p>Ayara stared at the jar. “I’m feeling way over my head at this point.”</p>

<p>“What, it’s just a jar of water from the freaking Underworld,” Lorelei snarked. “What’s the big deal?”</p>

<p>The Drow Blacksmith gave her a dirty look.</p>

<p>“Right, sorry,” the Hearth Spirit said contritely. “It’s just—the Underworld? Gods? They’re real?”</p>

<p>One of the more uncomfortable realizations brought about by The Reckoning was the existence of the divine. In the Systems, Gods were real. Clerics could obtain powers from their divine patrons, allowing them to use miracle Skills. Several gods had appeared, like Ephis and Ramnos, offering power to those who followed them.</p>

<p>Even more disturbing for some folks was that some followers of Earth’s religions, Christianity, Islam, and even Gnosticism, had been contacted and given similar offers. I knew a couple of wars had been waged in the Middle East due to that situation.</p>

<p>Ayara pensively tapped the top of the jar. I knew religion was a touchy subject for the Drow. They had become nomads due to religious persecution. They worshiped a goddess they referred to as the End; I had heard Ayara invoke her name several times.</p>

<p>“The gods are very real,” the Drow finally confirmed.</p>

<p>“The question is, how did this jar get here?” I gestured toward the busted steamer trunk. “The other stuff in there is almost a hundred and fifty freaking years old, and unlike the anvil, we can’t just pretend this jar became magical after The Reckoning.”</p>

<p>“So, what, there was magic on Earth before the System?” Lorelei frowned.</p>

<p>That threw everything Humanity knew about its history, or the System, into question.</p>

<p>I regarded Ayara for a second. “How do the Drow interpret the System? Humanity has about a couple thousand theories, but I doubt any of them are right.”</p>

<p>She gave me a troubled look. “We regard it as a gift from Umbralis, Queen of Peaceful Passings and Rightful Ends.”</p>

<p>“The whole <em>By the End </em>thing you keep muttering?” the Hearth Spirit guessed.</p>

<p>Ayara nodded. “On our home world, she was wrongly regarded as a goddess of death and suffering, which brought—”</p>

<p>She shook her head. “Nevermind. Just know that when we were on the verge of destruction, The Blessed End granted us the strength to protect our homes. When the danger became too great, she opened the paths to endless worlds, new lands like Earth.”</p>

<p>“The Dungeons,” Lorelei realized.</p>

<p>“Yes, a great gift,” Ayara agreed, before looking back at the jar. “Honestly, it might be best to hide this thing until Ishikana arrives. She might be able to explain how this jar got here and what it contains.”</p>

<p>The jar and its contents were valuable but dangerous. It was easy to imagine people like Mitch, who clearly had some kind of agenda in regard to me and my Lair, coveting it.</p>

<p>I handed it to Lorelei. “Can you stick this back in the basement? How about in the boiler room, somewhere people can’t reach it easily, like in the pipes.”</p>

<p>She gave me a mock salute, then flew out of the room.</p>

<p>“How long till Ishikana gets here?” I asked the Blacksmith.</p>

<p>Ayara looked uncertain. “I emailed her this morning. She had just arrived in New York. So, maybe three—four days?”</p>

<p>I grimaced. It sucked; I didn’t like waiting on someone else for answers.</p>

<p>“Guess we should finish cleaning out the basement then,” I said with a sigh.</p>

<p class="scene-break">***</p>

<p>Even mysterious jars and household cleaning could not save me from having Sunday dinner with my parents. My mother was upset since it had been a while since my last visit, so she was rather insistent we come by that evening.</p>

<p>She had extended an invitation to Lorelei, so I decided to bring Ayara as well. I didn’t want to leave the Drow Blacksmith home by herself, even though I was sure she’d spend the evening at her forge.</p>

<p>Thus, freshly showered and shaved, I joined Ayara and Lorelei in the foyer.</p>

<p>Ayara looked nervous. Forgoing her usual athletic wear, she was wearing a pair of black slacks and a matching short-sleeve blouse. Her hair was pulled up in a simple bun, making her look unusually serious.</p>

<p>“Am I dressed well enough?” she asked.</p>

<p>I leaned up and kissed her cheek. “You look beautiful. As do you, Lorelei.”</p>

<p>The Hearth Spirit chuckled, knowing that I had complimented her to cut off any potential complaints of favoritism. Able to brush off the humid weather, she had crafted a heavy dress, reminiscent of something from a period piece.</p>

<p>“You’ve been binging Uptown Abbey again, haven’t you?” I asked archly.</p>

<p>She giggled. “What can I say, that show has a real eye for fashion!”</p>

<p>I rubbed my face. “What happened to William Shatner?”</p>

<p>Ayara tilted her head. “William who?”</p>

<p>“Right.” I pointed out the door. “Out to the truck, let’s go. If we’re late for dinner, I’ll never hear the end of it!”</p>

<p>Lorelei ended up riding on top of the truck cab. We could periodically hear her cheer and shout as we rode around the outskirts of town toward my folks’ place.</p>

<p>She seemed to appreciate it when I gunned the engine crossing over the hills and ridges.</p>

<p>My folks lived just off Ashling State’s campus. My mom was a nurse, she worked at the college’s clinic three days a week while my dad was a high school teacher. The System had marked them as a Medic and Mentor respectively, but I doubted they had looked at their stat sheets more than twice since The Reckoning.</p>

<p>Ayara was apprehensive as we parked in the driveway.</p>

<p>I took her hand. “It’ll be fine, trust me.”</p>

<p>Taking a deep breath, she walked toward the front door like a soldier marching toward the front.</p>

<p>The Blacksmith stood stiffly on the porch as I knocked. My dad opened the door, my mom right behind him.</p>

<p>“Hello! I am Drax’Ayarellum of House Druxsellaic!” Ayara said, giving a short bow. “I thank you for your hospitality!”</p>

<p>Dad stared wide-eyed.</p>

<p>Lorelei floated through the wall.</p>

<p>“Hi, Charlie! Hi, Jeanie!” she said, using my parents’ first names. “Thanks for having us over!”</p>

<p>“Hello, Lorelei.” My mom smiled at me. “Brent, who’s your new friend?”</p>

<p>I stepped inside and ushered the Drow after me; she had to duck to make it through the doorway.</p>

<p>“Evening mom. This is Ayara.”</p>

<p>“It’s a pleasure,” the Blacksmith said, bowing again.</p>

<p>“No need to be so formal,” my dad said, regaining some of his composure. “I’m Charles, this is my wife, Jean. It’s nice to meet you.”</p>

<p>“Thank you.” Ayara still looked tense. “I appreciate the hospitality of your house and the gracious offer for supper.”</p>

<p>“It’s just pot roast,” my mom answered. “Still, I’m glad Brent warned me that he was bringing an extra guest with him, so I could set another spot at the table.”</p>

<p>Lorelei floated toward the dining room. “It smells great!”</p>

<p>Putting my hand on the small of Ayara’s back, I pushed her toward the dining room.</p>

<p>“It’s fine, trust me,” I whispered to her. “Just relax and be yourself.”</p>

<p>She gave me a brittle smile.</p>

<p>Dinner was a pleasant affair. Avoiding the more awkward questions, my parents asked Ayara about her job and then about her Blacksmithing. They also asked me about the progress on Rotwood Manor. I didn’t mention anything about the anvil or the jar or mystic water. Instead, I kept the conversation light and mundane.</p>

<p>The meal, pot roast with peas and mashed potatoes on the side, was delicious. Ayara and I weren’t cooks, and Lorelei didn’t even eat, so our meals tended to be pretty simple. The Drow and I normally took turns making budget-friendly meals in Rotwood’s stripped-down kitchen. We normally went for expediency and ease of cleanup over other considerations.</p>

<p>“This is fantastic,” I told my mother as I finished off my second serving.</p>

<p>“Indeed, thank you again for having us,” Ayara agreed.</p>

<p>Lorelei sat at the end of the table and grinned. “It smelled great!”</p>

<p>“I still feel bad I couldn’t serve you anything,” my mom objected.</p>

<p>The Hearth Spirit waved her hand. “Don’t worry, I know Brent enjoyed it, I could sense his happiness, which is enough for me!”</p>

<p>I cleared the table, and Ayara volunteered to help my mother wash the dishes. Lorelei snuck off to the living room to look through my baby album and childhood photos again. I noticed my dad slipped out the back door, a guilty look on his face.</p>

<p>Joining him in the backyard, he swore me to secrecy as he lit up a smoke from a pack of stale cigarettes.</p>

<p>“Your mother thinks I’ve completely quit, but I still have one once a week,” he confessed.</p>

<p>“I won’t tell a soul,” I promised.</p>

<p>We stood under the old maple tree that grew in the corner of the yard, just enjoying the quiet evening. Its leaves were turning yellow, a clear sign that fall was coming, but it was still pleasant outside.</p>

<p>“So…” My father took a drag. “Two women?”</p>

<p>I nodded. “Yeah.”</p>

<p>None of us had said anything about our relationship over dinner, but I hadn’t been hiding my affection for Ayara or Lorelei either.</p>

<p>My dad glanced at me. “They both know?”</p>

<p>I snorted. “Of course. I know it’s not traditional—but it’s how things worked out.”</p>

<p>He nodded. “The world is changing. Us older folks are still clinging on to what we know, but you kids are blazing a new trail. I’m excited to see what you find.”</p>

<p>He ground out his smoke and hid it under a broken flowerpot. “Just don’t tell your mother, I think she’d have a stroke.”</p>

<p>“She’s stronger than you think,” I said with a chuckle. “As you said, we know not everyone will get it, but I’m committed to them, and they’re committed to me.”</p>

<p>“Good.” He headed back toward the house. “Come on. Your mom and I picked up a pound cake from the bakery.”</p>

<p>We sat down for coffee and dessert after the kitchen was cleaned up. My mom brewed an extra strong cup and handed it to Lorelei. Even though she couldn’t drink it, my mom knew the Hearth Spirit enjoyed the aroma.</p>

<p>The pound cake was excellent. Ayara gasped in surprise at how sweet it was, swearing she’d never had something so good before.</p>

<p>Slowly the conversation turned to local gossip. One of the teachers my dad worked with was retiring. My mom told us about life on the campus of Ashling State and the hijinks the seniors were getting up to.</p>

<p>The college was gearing up for the town festival. The science school was organizing an eclipse viewing party, wrangling up every piece of polarized glass they could find.</p>

<p>“They’re holding the fall science fair too,” she explained. “First prize is a big old trophy and a month’s free meal tickets at the cafeteria.”</p>

<p>Changing the topic a bit, I asked my folks what they knew about the goat farm at the edge of town. They both shook their heads, having been completely unaware of its existence up until that point.</p>

<p>“You should be careful though,” my mom warned me, a slight look of worry crossing her face. “They found a dead man up in the hills this morning, a local.”</p>

<p>Lorelei’s eyes went wide. “Really?”</p>

<p>My mom nodded. “Yeah, the police have no clue what’s going on, but Beth, my cousin, works as a police dispatcher. She said it looked like Satanists! They found the poor man, hung upside down from a tree with all his blood drained out!”</p>

<p>“Creepy,” I muttered.</p>

<p>My dad pulled out a copy of the Sunday paper, which had the murder as the headline story. The victim, Larry Gnotts, was a dropout from Ashling State. He’d stuck around town, doing odd jobs and occasionally getting in trouble with the law. I didn’t recognize his name, but the blurry picture that accompanied the article was unmistakable.</p>

<p>It was the drunk man I’d kicked off my property, the one I’d seen with my neighbor, Herb the goat farmer.</p>
