<p>There was a small patio at the back of Brown’s house. Based on the woodpile and the ax-chipped stump, we had arrived while the former college professor had been chopping wood. A stack of split logs ran along the tree line.</p>

<p>Brown had gone inside the house without saying a word. Ayara and I sat around an old, rusty patio table while Ishikana rolled over a large stump to rest on. Lorelei flittered about but eventually settled by my shoulder.</p>

<p>When Brown returned, he came out with a tray of cups and a pitcher of water. A thick portfolio was stuck under his arm.</p>

<p>“I don’t have much in the way of hospitality, but I figured you might be thirsty. It’s a long drive from Ashling Grove,” he said.</p>

<p>“We brought scones!” the Hearth Spirit said as she put down the bakery box.</p>

<p>Ishikana quickly grabbed a pastry for herself, but the rest of us sat around, an awkward silence building between us.</p>

<p>“So,” I finally said. “You were the local historian and folklore expert at Ashling State?”</p>

<p>Brown nodded. “I taught there for nearly twenty years and retired in 2021. I just got tired of—well, everything.”</p>

<p>He gave a small huff. Looking at him more closely, I could sense the weariness coming off him, a long, malign exhaustion. Identify returned that he was a level five Mentor and level two Laborer, but that was about it.</p>

<p>“How long have you been living at Rotwood Manor?” he asked.</p>

<p>I did some mental math. “About five months. I moved in at the beginning of June.”</p>

<p>“Then you’ve survived longer than most owners.” He gave a dark laugh.</p>

<p>A tick of anger ran through me. “What are you getting at?”</p>

<p>He calmly looked through the bakery box and picked out a scone. “Just what I mean. Your house, your land, it’s cursed. And I don’t mean that as an insult. Just a simple fact. Dark deeds have happened there, staining the land. Everyone who moves in there comes to tragedy.”</p>

<p>“Except Delta Iota Kappa,” I replied.</p>

<p>He frowned. “<em>Especially </em>them. They might be the most damned of you all.”</p>

<p>“Cut the mystic crap,” Ayara said, taking a large bite from her scone.</p>

<p>Ishikana pulled out her own pile of papers, including the newspaper clippings and pages she’d recovered from the steamer trunk. She had restored several new documents, which were placed at the top of the stack.</p>

<p>One was an old photo. It had gone sepia-brown with age, the details little more than ghostly lines. It showed a giant of a man whose stature might have put Ayara’s to shame. He was wearing a stern expression. An equally serious woman sat next to him as they grimaced at the camera.</p>

<p>Brown leaned in and squinted. “That’s the clearest photo of Eustice and Marabelle Phelps I’ve ever seen. Did you find it on your property?”</p>

<p>“So that <em>is </em>him,” I said, studying the photo. “I didn’t know he was married though. We’ve only found random references to him, indicating he was a blacksmith.”</p>

<p>Brown sorted his own papers. “Before I left Ashling State, I made copies of everything I could. I didn’t trust the college or the town elders not to bury the truth. Guess it was a smart move after all.</p>

<p>He pulled out a photocopy. It was a grainy image, showing the same man, but several years older. His face was lined with grief.</p>

<p>“Eustace Phelps was the town’s blacksmith and tinkerer during the 1870s and 1880s,” Brown explained, tapping his finger against the photocopy. “I’ve never been able to locate his homestead, but I believe it was close to Rotwood Manor.”</p>

<p>I thought of the junkyard and its tumbled-down walls, all hidden by overgrowth and garbage.</p>

<p>The professor pulled another photocopy from the pile. It was an article from the Ashling Gazette.</p>

<p>“Eustace made a living forging ax heads, horseshoes, and other odds and ends the folks of Ashling Grove needed,” he explained. “He fixed broken wagon wheels, mended pots and pans, even did a few construction jobs.”</p>

<p>“He was a handyman,” I realized, a worrying parallel to my own life.</p>

<p>Brown nodded. “His wife passed away in 1882. That’s where all the stories begin.”</p>

<p>He pulled out another photocopy. I saw it was an obituary for Marabelle Phelps. It was short, only a few lines, noting she had died after a long battle with a <em>wasting illness</em>.</p>

<p>Brown regarded us. “How familiar are you with the folktales and legends surrounding Ashling Grove?”</p>

<p>“I didn’t know we even had any,” I admitted.</p>

<p>Lorelei shook her head. The town had a lot of weird traditions, but hardly any rumors beyond the harrowing stories told about Rotwood Manor, or so we thought.</p>

<p>Brown sighed, rubbing his brow. “I’m not surprised, really. The town elders have gone to great efforts over the years to obscure the past. They started in the 1940s when Ashling State was first founded. It only grew worse after The Reckoning, when the Dungeon sprung up. They wanted everyone to forget, to stop asking <em>why</em> a town as small and insignificant as ours would suddenly have a Dungeon.”</p>

<p>Ayara frowned. “It never made much sense to me. I asked the college staff a few times when I was there on delves. They just brushed it off as an oddity, a weird quirk of the System.”</p>

<p>“In truth, there have been dark legends and unsightly rumors about Ashling Grove, about the land it sits upon, from centuries,” Brown told us. “Even before the first white settlers arrived, the native peoples, the Susquehannock, avoided the area. I haven’t been able to discover much of their folklore, they were largely wiped out or exiled over the intervening years, but they avoided even stepping foot in the valley where our town resides.”</p>

<p>“And let me guess, the first colonial settlers found tragedy as well,” I promoted.</p>

<p>He nodded, patting a pile of handwritten notes. “Nothing official, mind you. Living in the middle of nowhere in the 1700s and 1800s was dangerous, but the natural resources in the area kept drawing people in. The local economy was based on hard, hazardous work: farming, lumbering, and mining.”</p>

<p>I thought of the vision I’d experienced in the cave, of the phantoms offering sacrifice before the blazing bonfire. I’d seen settlers and revolutionary soldiers dancing around the flames. I asked Brown about it.</p>

<p>“I haven’t heard anything about that, but it wouldn't surprise me,” he answered. “Ashling Grove is a town built on secrets and forgotten tragedy. But as for the stories? They really didn’t start until Eustace Phelps.”</p>

<p>Brown pulled out another bundle of notes. “Eustace had started life as a pastor. He was an itinerant preacher, traveling across the state until he happened upon our fair town and met Marabelle Smith.”</p>

<p>“Love at first sight?” Lorelei asked.</p>

<p>Brown shrugged. “It’s impossible to say, I can only go by the historical record. I have documentation about Phelps graduating with a degree in theology from a school in Philadelphia, bits here and there about his preaching and sermons, and then the couple’s wedding announcement a few years later.”</p>

<p>He showed us all this, mostly from copies or notes he made. They only gave scant details of Eustace Phelps's life, all commentary, no color. I looked back at the photo of the doomed couple.</p>

<p>“Things changed with his wife’s death, at least according to the legends, the oral traditions the town's elders tried to wipe out,” Brown continued. “The blacksmith was heartbroken with Marabelle’s illness, and utterly shattered by her death. He fell apart, gave up his work and became a drunk. People said he'd go out into the woods at night and scream at the moon and the stars, demanding God bring back his wife.”</p>

<p>Ishikana frowned but said nothing.</p>

<p>“It wasn’t until there was a solar eclipse, which I pinned down to 1883, that he finally got an answer to his demands,” Brown said. “Each story is different, but they all agree that during an eclipse, Phelps once again went into a fury spurred on by grief and liquor. Some say he made a deal with a demon; others say it was the Devil himself. I even heard a version where he shook hands with an angel. But they all agree he made a bargain with something during the eclipse.”</p>

<p>“What happened next?” Lorelei asked in a hushed tone.</p>

<p>Brown grunted. “The stories divide. Some say he began with animal sacrifice, splitting open animals’ skulls with his hammer and anvil. Others claim he attacked travelers, lone individuals on the road, dragging them off to hidden shrines in the woods. All of them put blood on his hands.”</p>

<p>I remembered the vision by the bonfire circle, the man with the hammer sacrificing a goat before the roaring blaze.</p>

<p>“I’m guessing this story doesn’t have a happy ending,” Ayara growled.</p>

<p>“No, it doesn’t.” Brown sat back in his seat. “Eventually, Phelps learned the entity he was dealing with wasn’t going to keep its word, it wasn’t going to bring his wife back. So, he banished it and threw it back to the dark beyond that it had crawled out from. The ritual cost him his life, the price for his transgressions.”</p>

<p>“How did he banish it?” I demanded.</p>

<p>“I don’t know, there’s no definitive answer,” he responded. “Most, though, say it was through holy water, blessed by the man himself with the last spark of his faith.”</p>

<p>I flashed back to the cave. Phelps had been carrying a huge jug, demanding the creature in the flames keep its word, or else. When it had refused, he had shattered the jug.</p>

<p>Had we found the broken remains of that jug in the Dungeon? I had felt a trace of mana on them, a strange mix of elements.</p>

<p>Maybe it had held holy water, but not the kind talked about in the tales.</p>

<p>“Is there anything in the old folk tales about the River Styx?” I demanded.</p>

<p>Brown looked surprised. He quickly shuffled through his papers.</p>

<p>“There was one story, a weird one, I dug up a dime-store novel from a local publisher in the 1930s. I dismissed it as a spooky story, a crass ghost tale.” He found what he was looking for. “In that version, the storyteller claims Phelps made a deal with a god of the underworld, Hades. When Hades reneged on their bargain, Phelps used water from the River Styx to banish him back to the underworld, imprisoning him in his dark realm.”</p>

<p>We had found broken jug shards in the Dungeon. Had that been the same one from the vision, had it contained water from the River Styx?</p>

<p>Maybe it hadn’t held all the water Phelps had retrieved. Maybe, just maybe, he had saved a small portion as a backup plan if he had been unable to banish the entity. If so, what did that mean for us, could we use it the same way?</p>

<p>Even with answers, we had too many questions.</p>

<p>“Phelps’ story ends, but the legends continue,” Brown said. “Even if the town elders buried the reason behind it, the town’s eclipse festival is a direct result of these stories and legends. The land Phelps’ home sat on, where he conducted his dark rituals, remained the town’s black spot.”</p>

<p>“And eventually, an out-of-towner, Patrick Ganlts, decided to build his mansion there,” I concluded.</p>

<p>“I doubt he knew about Phelps and the tale behind him,” Brown said. “Ganlts arrived in Ashing Grove in 1900 and quickly became the richest, most powerful man in town thanks to his lumber and mining interests. He bought a huge tract of land, which included the site of Phelps’s home.”</p>

<p>“We’ve already researched and confirmed his efforts to mine the land came to naught,” the Scholar said, flipping through her research.</p>

<p>“Ganlts wasn’t the type of man to be put off by a few deaths. His biography painted him much like his peers, the robber barons of the early twentieth century,” Brown agreed. “He was driven, uncompromising, and downright cruel when it came to business.”</p>

<p>Reaching under the table, I took Lorelei’s hand and squeezed it.</p>

<p>“When people began to whisper he had made a mistake in buying the land, he doubled down and built a huge mansion, Rootwood Manor, on the site,” Brown continued.</p>

<p>“We know enough from there,” I said gruffly, unwilling to have Lorelei once again endure the history of the Ganlts family. “Can we jump ahead? What do DIK and Baphoment have to do with all of this?”</p>

<p>“Baphomet?” Brown scoffed. “That’s the name DIK came up with for the dark entity Phelps had entreated with, but I believe it to be a misnomer. It’s a bit of occultist bullshit, to be frank.”</p>

<p>DIK had been around since the 1970s, but they hadn’t purchased and moved into Rotwood Manor until 1992. By that point, the house had gone through multiple owners, only some of which I knew about.</p>

<p>“Things started going wrong right away,” Brown groused. “I was an undergrad then, part of the history department. Delta Iota Kappa was an academic fraternity, several members had graduated with Summa Cum Laude over the years. But after moving into Rotwood Manor, they started caring less and less about school and their grades and more about throwing the biggest, wildest parties.”</p>

<p>“Why did they move into Rotwood Manor in the first place?” I asked. “It’s awfully far away from the campus.”</p>

<p>“I’m not sure,” Brown admitted. “Maybe they were drawn in by the dark rumors about the place—maybe it was just the ridiculously cheap price it supposedly sold for? Either way, soon after moving in they set about becoming the biggest party frat in town.”</p>

<p>“And started holding ceremonies dedicated to Baphomet,” I growled.</p>

<p>He showed us various disciplinary records he’d copied from the college’s records. DIK had been in trouble for a long, long time before their expulsion.</p>

<p>“Not at first,” Brown warned me. “I have no idea how they came up with the name <em>Baphomet</em>, maybe they pulled it from the news of the Satanic Panic of the 1980s? I interviewed several former frat members, those who graduated or were expelled before the frat’s dissolution in 2008. Most refused to talk, but those that did said it started as a <em>joke</em>.”</p>

<p>“A joke?” Ayara sputtered.</p>

<p>“That’s what they claimed.” Brown held up his hands. “They found some old notes, a bit of <em>ritual magic</em> left behind by a previous owner. This was before The Reckoning, nobody <em>believed</em> magic was real. So, they decided to conduct the ritual one day, during a town festival. From there…”</p>

<p>He shook his head. “They wouldn’t tell me anything else, and everyone after that point is frantically loyal to DIK, even to this day. But something happened. I believe they made contact with whatever entity Eustace Phelps summoned, and it offered them the same deal it offered the blacksmith. Blood and sacrifice to make all their wishes come true.”</p>

<p>Brown pulled out another set of documents. I recognized them as a clean copy of the one Ishikana and I had found. Apparently, he had managed to copy it before it was water damaged.</p>

<p>“February 7th, 2008. During the height of the town festival, several scantily clad and terrified women rushed into town. They claimed to be members of an out-of-state sorority, invited by Delta Iota Kappa for a party,” Brown said, tapping the papers. “Instead, they found themselves pulled into a black mass officiated by Mitch Felch, then president of the frat.”</p>

<p>“It was hard to get an exact story from them, but they all spoke of a bonfire, animal sacrifices, and blood rites,” Brown said, pulling out a stack of photos. “Some claimed they heard a voice, like the call of the devil, speaking to them.”</p>

<p>They showed terrified, bloodstained women, all huddled in blankets. They all had thousand-yard stares like they’d marched through hell.</p>

<p>“When they tried to leave, Mitch threatened to sacrifice them to Baphomet, the <em>dark lord </em>the rites were dedicated to,” Brown continued. “Thankfully, for them, there was some kind of accident, and the front porch caught fire. While Delta Iota Kappa scrambled to put it out, the sorority girls escaped and ran to town. From there—well, I imagine you know most of the story.”</p>

<p>“And what, Mitch and the DIK boys didn’t go to jail?” I demanded.</p>

<p>Brown shrugged. “Mitch Felch’s family was well off, still is as far as I know. The town elders were quick to bury the truth lest rumors of Satan worship stain the town’s reputation. Everything was swept under the rug.”</p>

<p>“Fuck.” I clenched my fist.</p>

<p>“Something doesn’t make sense to me,” Ayara interrupted. “Mitch and the others were kicked out of school, but they still seem obsessed with the manor and getting rid of us. Why didn’t they just buy it themselves, especially if Mitch’s family has money?”</p>

<p>Brown chuckled. “That’s another interesting twist in the story. Would you be surprised to hear that Mitch Felch and various Delta Iota Kappa members <em>did</em> try to buy the house after The Reckoning, but the deal always fell through?”</p>

<p>“After The Reckoning?” Ishikana leaned in, clearly intrigued.</p>

<p>“Mitch Felch was sent out of state by his family after his expulsion. After rehab, he managed to finish school elsewhere,” Brown explained. “By the time he came back, the System was firmly in place. He immediately tried to buy Rotwood Manor, using another fellow DIK alumni to make the purchase. That man was killed a week later, rogue Kobold attack while walking home from work.”</p>

<p>Such things weren’t unheard of, right after The Reckoning. The world was still new to Monsters, and organized guilds or hunting groups didn’t exist. Still, it seemed too much like a coincidence to me.</p>

<p>“Next, Mitch tried to buy it himself, using one of his family’s car dealerships as collateral,” Brown noted. “It burned down. Again and again, Delta Iota Kappa tried to get back into the house, and each time they were thwarted by frankly—strange circumstances.”</p>

<p>“Was the System working against them?” I wondered.</p>

<p>“I don’t know,” Brown replied. “I’ve tried to keep track of Mitch Felch, tried to warn people away from purchasing Rotwood Manor or getting involved with its dark history.”</p>

<p>“Strange then, that we've never spoken before,” I said accusingly.</p>

<p>The professor sighed, suddenly looking a hundred years older. His expression fell, and the light in his eyes dimmed.</p>

<p>“I grew tired,” he finally admitted. “The town never approved of me looking into its past. Even being a tenured professor couldn’t protect me from the little hints, the denied opportunities, the fucking bullshit. And I did warn people, I warned everyone who bought Rotwood Manor, but they never believed me. It was hard, thankless work and I eventually—just gave up. I quit my job, moved up here, and washed my hands of the whole thing. Until your call, I hadn’t heard anything about Rotwood Manor in years.”</p>

<p>I pointed to the papers. “Yet you never forgot.”</p>

<p>“No,” he said, bowing his head. “I never could. I’m sorry you’re stuck in this mess. I know another eclipse is coming up. Even if Mitch Felch and the rest of Delta Iota Kappa can’t gain access to the manor grounds and whatever devils it possesses, I’m sure he will keep up his blood sacrifices. My recommendation, just run, leave the house before the next eclipse.”</p>

<p>“That’s never going to happen,” I said, once again taking Lorelei’s hand.</p>

<p>“Then what <em>are</em> you going to do?” Brown asked.</p>

<p>“We’re going to fight,” Ayara answered for me.</p>

<p>“Damn right,” I growled.</p>
