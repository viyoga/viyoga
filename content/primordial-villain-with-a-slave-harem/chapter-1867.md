<p>The escort returned to the Ravenshade ducal seat at dawn, and it returned without Count Corvane.</p>
<p>"His gates were shut, my lord." The escort captain stood at attention in the study and looked at a framed map instead of his duke. "The count sends his regrets. He is... indisposed. His men were courteous, my lord. Courteous, and... they were on the walls."</p>
<p>"Indisposed." Tharion repeated the word from behind his desk, level. The level was work.</p>
<p>"There is more. The ferry village across from his seat reports wagons on the count’s roads past midnight. Heavy ones, moving without lanterns. And the record-house chimneys... They were still burning when we rode out, my lord."</p>
<p>The last word reached the bottom of the duke’s stomach before his face moved, and his hands flattened slowly on the desk.</p>
<p>The count had built his whole service on executing before a thought finished forming, always a step ahead of the whistle. Now the whistle had blown, formally, under ducal seal, and the dog had heard it, and gone back to burying things.</p>
<p>The wagons were the count’s affairs moving somewhere darker; the chimneys, his papers becoming smoke. He was cleaning, unordered, at speed, and every fire he lit told anyone watching where to dig.</p>
<p>And Tharion could not order him to stop.</p>
<p>An order would exist. Ink, a courier, a road, a witness: a thread running upward from the count’s fires into this study.</p>
<p>The old arrangements had been built, over patient centuries, so that no thread ran upward, and that distance had kept him safe his entire tenure.</p>
<p>Now it worked exactly as designed, in the wrong direction: he could not reach down without leaving a handprint. He could only watch his own instrument panic from the far end of his own lands.</p>
<p>The escort captain had not moved. He stood where he had stopped, eyes still on the framed map, waiting on a duke who had not answered. Somewhere behind him the doors had opened, and the chancellor was already at the desk’s corner with a fresh sheet.</p>
<p>Fear, in the Duke of Ravenshade, had only one shape. It came out as orders.</p>
<p>"Riders to every road out of the count’s seat!" He was on his feet and the study had become a parade ground. "Name lists at every ferry and every gate. If Corvane travels, I hear of it before his boots cool. If a wagon leaves his lands, I want its weight, its cargo, and the name of the man driving it!"</p>
<p>The chancellor wrote at a speed that endangered the pen.</p>
<p>"And a survey party to our own border archives!" His fist came down, and the wax seals jumped in their tray. "If the crown wants a war of records, Ravenshade’s will be in perfect order. Perfect! Find me clerks who can read forty years of toll ink, and find them by noon."</p>
<p>The chancellor began a question, met his duke’s eyes, and retired it.</p>
<p>...</p>
<p>Back in the royal archive, mid-signature, Alexios reached into a desk drawer instead of the next stack.</p>
<p>What came out was old parchment, creased from months of intense copying: a crown warrant for the criminal Devil, in fading ink.</p>
<p>Alexios had signed it himself a lifetime of headaches ago, and had never once gotten around to canceling it.</p>
<p>He canceled it now: one stroke, void, stamped, initialed. He slid it across the desk without a word and went back to signing.</p>
<p>Quinlan flicked two fingers and the dead warrant lifted off the desk, riding a curl of air up to eye level while he read through his own crimes, warming at every charge. "It’s not even my birthday."</p>
<p>Alexios did not look up at him. He looked, briefly, at Ayame.</p>
<p>She grinned back, shameless as her lover.</p>
<p>"Take your allies and get out of my archive," said the king. "You’re thoroughly unpleasant."</p>
<p>"Aww..." said Quinlan, visibly hurt.</p>
<p>He left the way he’d arrived, the gate blooming mid-stride, Ayame taking his arm with one last fond glance at the stripped table.</p>
<p>Two aisles over, Black Fang tucked her own ledger containing centuries of criminal filing under her arm and walked out of the royal archive with it, committing one more crime in broad daylight.</p>
<p>No one stopped her.</p>
<p>...</p>
<p>The smoke report reached the royal archive before its seals had fully cooled.</p>
<p>"Fires over the Corvane seat since midnight," Alexios read aloud. "Still rising at dawn."</p>
<p>He crossed to the correspondence table and set the page down in front of the one person in the hall who had grown up reading Ravenshade smoke.</p>
<p>Morgana’s pen did not stop. "Record-house chimneys," she said. "Burning a full night." Her crimson eyes crossed the report once, unimpressed. "How utterly boring."</p>
<p>"The commission maps every blaze. Location, duration, order." Alexios took the page back. "Your house is drawing us a treasure map in smoke."</p>
<p>"My house?" The venom in the two words could have inconvenienced an antidote. "I merely donated the blood."</p>
<p>...</p>
<p>The Duke of Ravenshade gave his day to the crystal, and the day shrank with every call.</p>
<p>The count of the river marches answered from behind sealed archive doors, wobbling on every third word. "They were polite, Your Grace. They had a warrant. It had numbers in it. They knew the years, Your Grace. They knew them before they opened a single crate."</p>
<p>The next call was answered by a steward. He read his master’s regrets off a page his master had not written, and he read them twice, because he lost his place. Tharion set the crystal down before the third pass.</p>
<p>One call did not answer at all. Another came back with an apology and, between its lines, a new employer.</p>
<p>By late afternoon a single lever remained, and it was the best of them: Duskmere, the neighboring duchy across a shared border, the oldest friendship Ravenshade had.</p>
<p>He composed himself before making that call. He even sat.</p>
<p>The crystal lit at the second pulse.</p>
<p>"Tharion." Zaren of Duskmere’s voice arrived warm and worried at once. "Old friend. I wondered when you would call."</p>
<p>For the first time all day, the duke’s shoulders came down a fraction.</p>
<p>"Zaren. You’ve heard."</p>
<p>"The whole kingdom has heard."</p>
<p>"Then you understand what it means." Tharion leaned in, and the court voice came back into harness, measured and sure. "Today it’s Ravenshade’s tolls. Tomorrow it’s your marsh levies, and Thornhollow’s grain counts, and every ledger every house has kept since the Accord. The crown has forgotten what a great house is. We stand together, Zaren, or we kneel separately."</p>
<p>Silence held on the other end, and Tharion let it work.</p>
<p>Then, faint through the crystal, from somewhere sunlit on the other side of that border, came a woman’s voice.</p>
<p>"Dear? The winter roses opened this morning. Walk with me before the light goes."</p>
<p>Tharion knew the voice. Every duke alive knew the voice. Calienne Valorian, eldest daughter of King Alexios, wife of Duskmere.</p>
<p>The blood went out of the duke’s hands, and it went cold on the way. She had come in somewhere in the last minute of it, and the last minute was the part a king would want.</p>
<p>"A moment, my heart." Zaren’s words arrived muffled, a palm half over the crystal. The palm failed to muffle the smile in them.</p>
