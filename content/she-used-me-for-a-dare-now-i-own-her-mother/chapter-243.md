<p>March 29th, 2025 — Fourteen Days After the Event</p>

<p>Aethros Valley Perimeter — 0600 Hours</p>

<p>The specialized investigation team assembled at the fog boundary as dawn broke over the Himalayas.</p>

<p>Fifteen personnel. Five scientists. Five special operations soldiers with advanced technical training. Five support specialists. Each equipped with self-contained oxygen systems, hazmat-grade protective suits, multiple redundant communication devices, and equipment designed for the impossible task ahead.</p>

<p>Their mission was simple in concept, nightmarish in execution: Enter the fog. Locate any trace of the forty-seven operatives who had vanished. Retrieve survivors if possible. Bodies if necessary. Any evidence if nothing else remained.</p>

<p>Return within ninety-six hours or Phase Three protocols would activate.</p>

<p>The team leader... a civilian atmospheric physicist... ran final equipment checks while the military commander reviewed extraction procedures one last time. Behind them, satellite feeds transmitted their preparations to command centers across six continents.</p>

<p>World leaders watched. Waited. Hoped.</p>

<p>At precisely 0600 hours, the team crossed the fog boundary and disappeared into white silence.</p>

<p>***</p>

<p>Two Hundred Kilometers South — Simultaneous Operations</p>

<p>Evacuation orders had been quietly distributed across northern India and western China under the guise of "routine disaster preparedness exercises."</p>

<p>Millions of civilians in the designated zones received instructions: prepare emergency supplies, identify evacuation routes, monitor government communications channels.</p>

<p>The official explanation cited potential seismic activity and atmospheric instability.</p>

<p>The truth... that their governments were preparing for possible nuclear deployment if the investigation team failed... remained classified.</p>

<p>In New Delhi, emergency response coordinators mapped refugee routes that could move ten million people within seventy-two hours.</p>

<p>In Beijing, military logistics officers calculated supplies needed to evacuate entire provinces.</p>

<p>Neither nation expected to use these plans.</p>

<p>Both knew they might have no choice.</p>

<p>***</p>

<p>Aethros Valley Perimeter — 0800 Hours</p>

<p>Two hours after entry, something emerged from the fog.</p>

<p>The investigation team staggered out of the white wall, supporting each other, equipment damaged but functional, communication systems crackling back to life as they cleared the boundary.</p>

<p>Command centers worldwide erupted in controlled chaos. They’d returned. Against all expectations, against the pattern of forty-seven previous failures, this team had survived.</p>

<p>But the celebration died quickly when the count came through.</p>

<p>Fifteen had entered.</p>

<p>Fourteen returned.</p>

<p>And they carried one body between them.</p>

<p>***</p>

<p>Emergency Debriefing — United Nations Security Council</p>

<p>### Six Hours After Team Return</p>

<p>Secretary-General Silva stood before the Security Council, expression grim.</p>

<p>"The investigation team successfully penetrated the fog and returned after approximately two hours inside. However, we lost one member. Dr. Marcus Webb, atmospheric physicist from MIT. His body was recovered and is currently undergoing analysis."</p>

<p>He pulled up the team’s preliminary report.</p>

<p>"The team reports that visibility inside the fog was severely limited... approximately three meters maximum visual range regardless of equipment used. Thermal imaging, infrared, radar... all failed to penetrate beyond immediate proximity. They describe the environment as ’disorienting,’ with normal spatial reference becoming unreliable."</p>

<p>The Indian representative leaned forward. "And the previous teams? The forty-seven operatives who entered before?"</p>

<p>Silva’s expression darkened. "No survivors located. No bodies recovered. However..."</p>

<p>He pulled up images that made several delegates physically recoil.</p>

<p>"The team found extensive traces of biological matter. Blood. Tissue fragments. Scattered across a wide area approximately five hundred meters from the fog boundary. The pattern suggests... violent dispersal rather than organized remains."</p>

<p>The Chinese delegate’s voice was tight. "Are you saying the previous teams were killed inside? By what?"</p>

<p>"Unknown," Silva said. "The team encountered no hostile entities. No evidence of weapons discharge. No signs of combat. Just the biological remains and..."</p>

<p>He paused, clearly disturbed by what came next.</p>

<p>"...complete absence of intact bodies. As if the operatives were subjected to catastrophic systemic failure that left only... fragments."</p>

<p>Volkov spoke from the back, voice carrying its usual cold calculation. "And Dr. Webb? How did he die?"</p>

<p>Silva took a breath. "That’s where this becomes critical. Dr. Webb’s death occurred when he briefly removed his oxygen mask... approximately fifteen seconds of exposure to the fog’s atmosphere."</p>

<p>The room fell silent.</p>

<p>"The autopsy results are preliminary but conclusive," Silva continued. "Every blood vessel in Dr. Webb’s body ruptured simultaneously. Capillaries, veins, arteries... catastrophic systemic failure across his entire cardiovascular system. The pathologists describe it as similar to what might occur if someone were exposed to massive pressure differentials or..."</p>

<p>He pulled up the technical analysis.</p>

<p>"...subjected to energy influx far beyond human physiological capacity to process. One pathologist compared it to ’forcing a car battery’s worth of electricity through a watch battery.’ Dr. Webb’s body was attempting to intake and circulate something present in the fog’s atmosphere... something that overloaded his system completely within seconds."</p>

<p>The British representative spoke carefully. "Are you suggesting the fog itself is... composed of something that human physiology cannot tolerate?"</p>

<p>"Composed of or saturated with," Silva confirmed. "The samples collected by the team are still being analyzed, but preliminary results indicate the fog contains atmospheric concentrations of what can only be described as hyper-concentrated energy. Far beyond anything that should exist naturally."</p>

<p>"Energy?" The French delegate frowned. "What kind of energy?"</p>

<p>Silva looked uncomfortable. "The scientists are calling it ’exotic matter’ because they don’t have proper terminology. It doesn’t correspond to known forms of radiation or electromagnetic phenomena. But it’s present in such density that brief exposure causes human tissue to attempt rapid absorption... with catastrophic results."</p>

<p>He pulled up comparative images. "Dr. Webb’s internal damage is consistent with what we’d expect if someone tried to channel industrial power levels through uninsulated biological systems. His body tried to intake and process the energy present in the fog. His physiology couldn’t handle it."</p>

<p>"And the previous teams?" The Indian representative’s voice was barely above a whisper. "The forty-seven operatives who entered without protective equipment?"</p>

<p>Silva’s silence was answer enough.</p>

<p>They’d been exposed to the fog’s atmosphere without any protection. Their bodies had attempted to process energy concentrations that human physiology couldn’t survive.</p>

<p>The biological fragments scattered inside weren’t evidence of violence.</p>

<p>They were evidence of catastrophic systemic failure as human bodies literally tore themselves apart trying to absorb what existed in the fog.</p>

<p>***</p>

<p>Volkov stood slowly. "So we have confirmation. The fog is inherently lethal to human exposure. The previous teams died not from external threat but from the environment itself. And despite protective equipment, this team still lost one member and found only fragments of the others."</p>

<p>He looked around the chamber. "Given these findings, I return to my original assessment: whatever exists inside that fog is beyond our capacity to investigate safely. Continued attempts will only add to the body count."</p>

<p>The Chinese delegate interrupted. "But the team survived. With proper equipment, investigation is possible..."</p>

<p>"One team, two hours, one casualty," Volkov cut in. "And they found nothing but biological remains. No answers about what created the fog. No understanding of why it appeared. No insight into the phenomenon itself."</p>

<p>He gestured at the displayed images. "You asked for scientific investigation. You received confirmation that the environment is lethal and the previous teams are dead. What additional information do you expect to gain that justifies further risk?"</p>

<p>The Indian representative spoke firmly. "The team’s report isn’t complete. They were inside for only two hours. A longer expedition, with improved equipment based on what we’ve learned..."</p>

<p>"Will produce more casualties and identical results," Volkov said flatly. "The fog’s lethality is confirmed. Its resistance to investigation is demonstrated. The question is no longer ’can we investigate safely?’ The answer is clearly no. The question now is: what do we do about a lethal, persistent phenomenon we cannot safely approach, cannot understand, and cannot eliminate through conventional means?"</p>

<p>The chamber fell silent.</p>

<p>Because he was right.</p>

<p>The investigation had provided answers... just not the ones anyone wanted.</p>

<p>The fog was lethal. The previous teams were dead. And humanity’s best attempt at scientific investigation had cost another life while yielding nothing but confirmation of their helplessness.</p>

<p>Secretary-General Silva’s voice was heavy. "The Security Council will review the team’s complete findings over the next forty-eight hours. All member states will provide analysis and recommendations. We reconvene in three days to determine next steps."</p>

<p>He looked directly at Volkov. "Phase Three consideration remains on the table. But we will not rush to decisions based on incomplete data."</p>

<p>Volkov shrugged. "Of course, Secretary-General. Take your three days. Review your data. Debate your options."</p>

<p>His smile was cold. "But we all know where this ends. The only question is how many more people die before we accept the obvious conclusion."</p>

<p>He sat back down, expression unchanged, while around him delegates struggled with the realization that his "practical solution" was looking less extreme and more inevitable with each passing day.</p>

<p>***</p>

<p>Alex stared at the silver text as it continued materializing across the obsidian pages.</p>

<p>One team had returned. But the discovery was worse than silence.</p>

<p>The previous operatives were dead... their bodies torn apart by exposure to something in the fog that human physiology couldn’t survive. And the investigation team’s loss proved that even with protection, the environment remained lethal.</p>

<p>The world now faced a confirmed threat: a phenomenon that killed through mere exposure, that resisted all attempts at understanding, that showed no signs of dissipating or changing.</p>

<p>And Volkov’s nuclear option was no longer theoretical extreme.</p>

<p>It was becoming the only remaining choice.</p>

<p>The text shifted, new sections beginning to form.</p>

<p>’What happened next?’ he thought, leaning closer. ’Did they...’</p>

<p>He froze.</p>

<p>Something had shifted in the manor’s energy. Not threatening, but... different. Changed.</p>

<p>Alex stood slowly, the book remaining open on its pedestal, text still glowing softly as if waiting for his return.</p>

<p>’Victoria or Catherine,’ he realized, pulse quickening. ’One of them finished their breakthrough.’</p>
