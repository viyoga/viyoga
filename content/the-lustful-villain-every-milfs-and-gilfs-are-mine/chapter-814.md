<p>Rex turned his gaze toward her, his eyes cool and observant, dissecting the tremor in her voice even as he maintained his stoic facade.</p>
<p>"I am trying to understand," Mireya said, her voice gaining a sudden, sharp edge of intellectual desperation, "what the entity’s objective truly was."</p>
<p>"The change in how the Underlayer is governed, the start of the golem network, the planned movement of the civilian population... the careful choice of targets during the attack."</p>
<p>"It didn’t just attack; it built something. It constructed a precise, lethal mechanism for a single morning."</p>
<p>She turned her head slightly, her gaze falling back onto the cold, unyielding markers of the dead. "The outcome it produced was inconsistent with total annihilation."</p>
<p>"If the primary goal had been the erasure of the Apostle network, the earthquake protocol would have been triggered at full output in the opening minutes, not held in reserve."</p>
<p>"The golem network would have been a concentrated hammer striking the apostle positions, rather than a distributed web meant to catch and entangle them."</p>
<p>"The math doesn’t add up, Rex... It’s a mismatch of intent and execution."</p>
<p>Rex shifted his stance, his eyes following hers to the markers, his mind already running the simulations of the battlefield’s geometry.</p>
<p>"The engagement’s architecture suggests the primary goal was disruption and demonstration, rather than pure elimination," Rex said, his voice a low, rhythmic drone of logic. "The Underlayer didn’t want a graveyard; they wanted a reorganization."</p>
<p>"A destroyed network creates a power vacuum, a chaotic, unpredictable void that is difficult to govern. But a destabilized network?"</p>
<p>"A network that is broken, terrified, and stripped of its veteran core? That produces a reorganization."</p>
<p>"The reorganization is precisely what the governance transition required to take hold."</p>
<p>Mireya fell silent, the weight of his words hanging in the heavy, funeral air. She was a woman of intellect, and she knew he was right, which only made the truth more bitter.</p>
<p>"That is a very specific analysis," she murmured, her eyes narrowing.</p>
<p>"The forty-year archive covers the Underlayer’s historical operational methodology," Rex replied, his tone as unshakeable as the stone beneath them. "The pattern is consistent."</p>
<p>"They don’t strike to kill; they strike to reshape."</p>
<p>She finally turned to look at him, her eyes searching his face for a crack, a sign of the man behind the machine.</p>
<p>"You were on the outer perimeter during the engagement’s early phase," she stated, her voice dropping to a cautious, dangerous level.</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said.</p>
<p>"The geological substrate read," she continued, her gaze intensifying. "It pulsed through the perimeter."</p>
<p>"It wasn’t just a tremor; it was a broadcast... It went through the perimeter, Rex. Directly toward the center."</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said, his expression remaining an impenetrable mask.</p>
<p>A heavy, suffocating silence stretched between them. Mireya looked back at the markers, her hands clenching at her sides, her knuckles turning white.</p>
<p>"Eryndra and Calivara," she said, the names sounding like accusations in the quiet of the garden. "They were in Tremor’s direct operational radius for eleven minutes before the engagement’s final phase."</p>
<p>"Eleven minutes, Rex... In a combat zone of that intensity, eleven minutes is an eternity to be within a designated target’s radius without being directly, violently addressed."</p>
<p>"They were there, standing in the path of a god, and the god simply... looked past them."</p>
<p>Rex stared at the markers, his mind flashing back to the carnage to the way the earth had buckled and the bones of the fallen had snapped like dry kindling under the sheer weight of the seismic pressure. He remembered the way the dust had choked the air, masking the movements of the entity.</p>
<p>"Yes," he said, the word a simple, brutal acknowledgment of the fact.</p>
<p>"Tremor addressed Valentina and Morwenna," Mireya said, her voice trembling with a suppressed, righteous fury.</p>
<p>She still wouldn’t look at him, her eyes fixed on the names of the dead as if demanding an answer from the earth itself. "It addressed the apostle network’s coordination capacity."</p>
<p>"It targeted the brain and the command structure, and it did not specifically address the individuals who would have been the most efficient targets for pure elimination."</p>
<p>"It left the most capable warriors alive, yet it placed them in the exact path of the destruction." She paused, a shudder running through her. "I have been trying to work out why..."</p>
<p>"Why were they left to the mercy of the physics?"</p>
<p>"The governance transition needed the network’s senior coordination capacity neutralized," Rex said, his voice cutting through her emotion with the cold precision of a scalpel. "It didn’t need the junior practitioners; it needed the leaders."</p>
<p>"The specific targets that served the objective were the ones Tremor addressed... Eryndra and Calivara... they weren’t the targets."</p>
<p>"They were simply the ones in proximity to the contested space when the engagement’s physics finally resolved."</p>
<p>Mireya finally turned her head, her gaze locking onto Rex’s with a piercing, unsettling intensity.</p>
<p>"In proximity to the contested space when the engagement’s physics resolved," she repeated.</p>
<p>The words came out in a flat, hollow cadence, not the tone of someone questioning his logic, but the tone of a woman gripping a sharp blade, testing its edge to see if it was truly keen enough to cut. She was holding his words in her mind, weighing them against the visceral, bloody memory of the battlefield.</p>
<p>Rex did not flinch. He held her gaze with the terrifying, steady patience of a predator or a machine, waiting to see if she would strike him with her suspicion or sink into his logic.</p>
<p>"I do not know," Mireya said finally, her voice barely a whisper above the rustle of the ancient trees, "whether the analysis you are giving me is accurate, or whether it is simply the analysis that is most useful for this specific morning."</p>
<p>She turned her back on him, her gaze returning to the cold markers of the dead. "I am choosing to accept it, Rex."</p>
<p>"Because the alternative is a different kind of grief, a grief that requires a reckoning with intent rather than accident, and I do not have the reserve to carry that weight today."</p>
<p>Rex said nothing. He offered no reassurance, no comfort, and no defense. He simply stood there, an unmoving pillar of fact.</p>
<p>"But I am going to come back to it," she added, her voice hardening with a quiet, dangerous resolve.</p>
<p>"I know," Rex replied. It wasn’t a promise; it was a calculation of her character.</p>
<p>With a final, sharp exhale, she retreated, walking back to the fractured edge of the group to rejoin the others.</p>
<p>In the western section of the garden, where the markers for the non-academy and non-apostle civilians were laid, the atmosphere was different. It was less structured, more raw.</p>
<p>There, amidst the rows of the common dead, sat Iris. Her coffin was not the small, delicate vessel of a child, nor the unremarkable box of a nameless laborer.</p>
<p>It was a well-crafted wooden structure, a testament to a respect that had been summoned in haste but executed with profound care.</p>
<p>Iris was kneeling before it, her body hunched as if under the weight of the very sky. Her hands were pressed flat against the wood, her knuckles white, her fingers digging into the grain as if she could pull the soul back through the lid.</p>
<p>The sound she made was not a cry or a sob; it was a low, rhythmic keening, a sound of grief so vast and so primal that it seemed to exceed the capacity of a human throat to contain it. It was the sound of a soul being stretched to the breaking point.</p>
<p>Rex watched her from a distance. He waited, calculating the social and emotional variables, until he saw Aurelia move toward her.</p>
<p>He waited until Aurelia had offered what comfort a living person could possibly provide to a person who was drowning in the dead. Only then did Rex begin his approach.</p>
<p>He moved through the garden, a silent shadow cutting through the solemnity, until he reached Iris’s side.</p>
<p>He did not offer words. Words were often just noise used to fill the terrifying silence of loss. Instead, he descended, crouching at the level of the coffin with a controlled, deliberate grace.</p>
<p>He reached into the folds of his attire and produced the flowers he had been carrying. He placed them at the base of the wooden structure, not as a traditional tribute to the departed, nor as a sentimental gesture toward the grieving woman.</p>
<p>To Rex, this was a ritual of acknowledgment. He placed them for Veylor because he understood that for those standing on the periphery of a tragedy, the duty was not to redirect the grief but to validate the absolute, crushing reality of the object that caused it.</p>
<p>Iris’s keening faltered. Her shoulders shuddered, and her eyes, red-rimmed and hollowed by exhaustion, drifted downward.</p>
<p>Iris looked at the flowers.</p>
