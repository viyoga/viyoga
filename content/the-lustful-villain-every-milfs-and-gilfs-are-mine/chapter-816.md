<p>Iris looked up at him, and for a fleeting second, the rigid, assassin-like structure of her composure faltered. It wasn’t a collapse but a subtle shift, the way a tectonic plate settles after a massive upheaval.</p>
<p>It wasn’t forgiveness, nor was it the resolution of her grief; it was the quiet, heavy relief that comes when a person realizes they no longer have to perform a version of sadness that the world expects of them. They were finally allowed to simply be in the wreckage.</p>
<p>"I know you could have let the meteor land," she said.</p>
<p>Her voice was low, barely a ripple in the heavy morning air. She spoke it not as an accusation but as a cold fact she had been dissecting since she watched the sky burn from the eastern building line.</p>
<p>"The mathematics of your arrival were precise, Rex..."</p>
<p>"Based on your trajectory and velocity, your intervention was not a requirement for survival... Tremor was already past the point of redirection."</p>
<p>"You could have arrived a second later, watched the impact, and the outcome would have been Tremor’s victory, a victory written in a crater of pulverized bone and scorched earth."</p>
<p>"You could have stood there, silent, and you wouldn’t have had to reveal a capability that would force everyone to start asking the kind of difficult questions that change the way they look at you."</p>
<p>Rex met her gaze, his expression unreadable, a calm sea beneath a storm of implications.</p>
<p>"But you caught it," she continued, her eyes searching his. "And I have been trying to run the variables to understand why."</p>
<p>"Given that the most efficient, most invisible version of your arrival required nothing from you but observation... why did you choose the most conspicuous path?" She looked at him with that flat, piercing read, the gaze of a woman who lived by the logic of the kill. "Actually... never mind."</p>
<p>"I don’t think I’m going to ask... I think the answer is something I would rather arrive at myself."</p>
<p>"That is probably wise," Rex replied.</p>
<p>A heavy silence descended, thick with the scent of damp earth and the lingering metallic tang of the previous day’s violence.</p>
<p>"Iris," Rex said, his voice cutting through the stillness with a sudden, grounding weight. "Whatever you need, whatever task, whatever burden, whatever debt, I will do it."</p>
<p>"Not to balance the scales, and not to pay for a life... But because you are someone who deserves to have someone willing to stand in the gap for you."</p>
<p>Iris froze. She turned her full attention to him, her ’assassin’s read’ running at maximum depth.</p>
<p>It was the look of a predator encountering a new kind of terrain, a landscape she had mapped a thousand times, only to find a mountain where there should have been a plain. She was seeing something in him that defied her internal models, something that wasn’t a tactical advantage or a social grace but a fundamental truth she wasn’t sure she was prepared to process.</p>
<p>She looked away, her eyes drifting back to the wooden coffin.</p>
<p>Then, she stood. The movement was slow and deliberate, as if her limbs were made of lead.</p>
<p>She reached down to the flowers Rex had placed at the base of the coffin. She picked up a single blossom, her fingers tracing the delicate, pale petals.</p>
<p>It was a white flower, a choice Rex had made instinctively, without knowing the man beneath the wood, yet he had chosen correctly. White was the color of the void left behind; white was the color of a silence that could not be filled.</p>
<p>She held it for a moment, a tiny, fragile thing against the backdrop of death, and then let it fall back against the dark grain of the coffin.</p>
<p>"The western cliffs," she said, her voice catching on the jagged edge of a memory. "He should have been buried there..."</p>
<p>"Amidst the salt and the gale. Not here, in this manicured cage."</p>
<p>"Aethelgard’s protocol is what it is," Rex said, offering the cold comfort of systemic reality.</p>
<p>"Aethelgard’s protocol," she spat the words, a flicker of bitter defiance returning to her eyes, "was designed for the general case."</p>
<p>"It was designed for the nameless, the many, and the statistics." She looked up at the ancient, gnarled trees of the garden, the grey, filtered light of the morning casting long, skeletal shadows across the graves. "He was not a general case."</p>
<p>"No," Rex said, the word heavy and final. "He was not."</p>
<p>She looked at him again. The air between them had changed.</p>
<p>The tension was no longer the sharp, electric heat of a confrontation but the profound, quiet pressure of two people who had looked into the abyss and survived it together. The dynamic had shifted irrevocably, though neither had moved an inch.</p>
<p>"I am going to visit the cliffs," she said, her voice regaining its strength, though it remained tempered by the morning’s sorrow. "Later..."</p>
<p>"When the ceremony is finished... and when the city begins to do what cities do after mornings like this one."</p>
<p>"When the blood is washed from the stones and the living begin to forget."</p>
<p>Rex offered no immediate response, the silence between them stretching like a taut wire.</p>
<p>"You could come," she said.</p>
<p>There was no tentative lilt to her voice, no social cushioning to make the invitation feel less heavy. It was a directive, delivered with the flat, unyielding directness of a soldier issuing a command or an assassin identifying a target. It was an invitation stripped of all pretense.</p>
<p>Rex turned his gaze toward her, his eyes narrowing slightly as he processed the offer. He wore the expression of a man receiving a variable he had not accounted for in his initial equations, a sudden, unexpected shift in the terrain that required immediate, deep-level analysis.</p>
<p>"Yes," he said.</p>
<p>Before he even had to consciously search for the data, the internal HUD of his consciousness flickered to life, projecting a translucent readout into his vision.</p>
<p>[Iris Morr Nightwing: Desire Level: 40/100]</p>
<p>Rex stood at the edge of the coffin, watching as Iris straightened her spine. She placed her hand on the wood one last time, a final, silent communion with the dead, and then stepped back.</p>
<p>She moved with the decisive grace of someone who had finished a task and was ready to move toward the next, leaving the stillness of the grave behind.</p>
<p>He thought about the western cliffs. He thought about the wind Veylor had sought out, the gale that had been the only thing capable of making the man feel alive.</p>
<p>He considered the profound, almost violent alchemy of the moment: how the raw, jagged edges of a person’s grief could, when met with the right presence, be forged into the foundation of something entirely new.</p>
<p>He thought about the number. 40/100.</p>
<p>He calculated the distance between forty and the next threshold, weighing the sheer, crushing weight of her sorrow against the potential for something else. He wondered if grief, in its most primal form, was the most durable building material in the human psyche.</p>
<p>He filed the thought away in a secure partition of his mind and smoothed his features, ensuring his expression remained at the precise, somber register required for a funeral.</p>
<p>Valentina stepped forward from the head of the ceremony. The air seemed to thicken as she took her place.</p>
<p>Her voice, when it rose, was not the polished, hollow tone of an administrator managing a public relations crisis. It was the voice of a woman who had lived long enough to have buried more people than she could name, a woman whose relationship with death was neither a performance nor an avoidance but a grim, honest acquaintance.</p>
<p>"We stand today in the shadow of a cost," Valentina began, her voice resonating through the quiet garden. "Aethelgard was not built on the strength of its walls alone but on the strength of the people who decided that what lay within those walls was worth the price of their own blood."</p>
<p>"For eight hundred years, we have defended this neutral ground... We have defended the idea that some things are worth more than the simple instinct to survive."</p>
<p>She paused, her gaze sweeping over the mourners.</p>
<p>"The cost of that defense is never abstract... It is not a number in a ledger..."</p>
<p>"It is the Academy students whose lives were cut short in the outer practice grounds when the golems breached the perimeter, lives that ended in a sudden, brutal cacophony of tearing metal and breaking bone..."</p>
<p>"It is the civilian fighters who, hearing the roar of the earthquake protocol, did not run for the shelters but ran toward the instability, choosing to stand as a shield for those they loved..."</p>
<p>"It is the Apostle network members who arrived at the engagement just as the mathematics of survival failed them, those who reached the plaza only to find the dust settling over a battlefield of broken bodies."</p>
<p>Her voice grew heavier, more resonant.</p>
<p>"We do not mourn them because they failed..."</p>
<p>"We mourn them because they succeeded in the most difficult task of all: they gave everything to defend the value of what we hold dear..."</p>
<p>"To die for a cause is a tragedy; to die because you chose to stand when the world was falling apart is a testament."</p>
<p>"We honor the cost, because the cost is the only true measure of the value of Aethelgard."</p>
