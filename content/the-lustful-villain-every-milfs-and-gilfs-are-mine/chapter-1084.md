<p>Rex moved. His footsteps were ghostly quiet as he carved a path through the forest of bowed heads and weeping shoulders.</p>
<p>He didn’t ask permission to enter her space, nor did he announce his approach. He simply navigated the minefield of grief until he reached her side.</p>
<p>When he came to a stop, his tall, broad-shouldered frame cast a long, protective shadow over her slight, trembling form. He didn’t reach out to touch her.</p>
<p>He didn’t offer the empty, hollow comfort of a platitude. He simply stood there, a silent, solid pillar of granite in the center of her collapsing world.</p>
<p>For a long time, the only sound was the distant, wet sniffle of a mourner and the wind rustling through the cemetery trees.</p>
<p>"The children," she whispered.</p>
<p>Her voice was hoarse, scraped raw by days of unspoken screams. She didn’t turn to look at him; she couldn’t.</p>
<p>"The ones caught in the field."</p>
<p>"The ones who... who stopped growing."</p>
<p>"The ones who died screaming because their bones couldn’t keep up with their skin."</p>
<p>"Restored," Rex said.</p>
<p>His voice was flat, devoid of inflection, but it carried the absolute certainty of fact. "The temporal disruption’s biological effect has been addressed."</p>
<p>Aurelia finally turned her head. Her eyes were bloodshot, the skin beneath them bruised with exhaustion.</p>
<p>"Restored? How?"</p>
<p>"The temporal substrate’s management function," Rex explained, his mind automatically accessing the precise technical framework of what he had done. "I acquired it during the engagement’s conclusion."</p>
<p>"It is a precise working at the cellular development level... I didn’t heal them, Aurelia."</p>
<p>"I corrected them... I forced their biological timelines to snap back into their proper sequence."</p>
<p>A silence fell between them, heavy as lead. She stared at him, searching his face for some sign of deception, some hint of the monstrous power he had wielded.</p>
<p>"You acquired it," she said, the realization dawning with a cold shiver. "You acquired the temporal authority."</p>
<p>"A local expression of it," Rex corrected immediately, unwilling to let her miscalculate his position. "Aethelgard specific."</p>
<p>"It is tied to this plane’s foundational architecture, not the general domain."</p>
<p>"It is a tool, not a godhead."</p>
<p>Aurelia didn’t blink. She seemed to be weighing the implications of the terrifying reality that Rex now held a key to the very fabric of their existence.</p>
<p>But her mind was too fractured to dwell on politics.</p>
<p>"And Kaeleth?" she asked, her voice dropping to a near inaudible murmur. "What of him?"</p>
<p>Rex’s jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Is no longer operationally present in Aethelgard’s foundational layer."</p>
<p>His mind flashed briefly to the violence of the end, the way Kaeleth’s divine essence had been systematically dismantled, the wet crunch of reality breaking under Rex’s will, and the absolute erasure of a threat that had once seemed insurmountable. "He has been removed."</p>
<p>Aurelia didn’t react to the news of the god’s death. She didn’t cheer or sigh with relief. Instead, she turned her gaze back to the garden’s gathered mourners.</p>
<p>Her eyes traced the makeshift markers, the small stones and flowers placed over the spots where the innocent had fallen.</p>
<p>"My son," she said.</p>
<p>The words weren’t a question. They were an accusation directed at the universe itself.</p>
<p>Rex said nothing. He knew that any words spoken now would be like throwing stones into an abyss.</p>
<p>"He chose," she said, her voice gaining a terrible, brittle strength. "He chose to walk toward that cliff."</p>
<p>"He chose to listen to the poison Kaeleth offered."</p>
<p>"He chose to believe he could control the storm." Her fingers curled into fists at her sides, shaking with suppressed rage and agony. "And he chose to come back here and do what he did."</p>
<p>"Those weren’t accidents, Rex... Those were his choices."</p>
<p>She looked down at the markers again, the graves of the people her son had inadvertently slaughtered.</p>
<p>"And the consequences of those choices," she said, her voice cracking like breaking ice, "are what they are."</p>
<p>Rex looked at the graves, then back at the woman who had raised a catastrophe. He saw the brutal mathematics of her grief: the cost of power, the price of free will, and the blood that paid for both.</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said.</p>
<p>Aurelia fell silent. The wind picked up, carrying the bitter scent of damp earth and funeral incense, but she didn’t flinch.</p>
<p>When she finally spoke again, her voice had shifted. The diplomatic cadence of a high-ranking official and the polished armor of her title—all of it fell away.</p>
<p>This was the register Rex had heard in the training halls: the raw, direct voice she used when she stripped away the identity of the Academy to speak to the human beneath.</p>
<p>"But," she said, and the word cut through the mourning like a blade, "he was also my child."</p>
<p>"And he was in a state that he did not put himself in alone."</p>
<p>Rex turned his head to meet her gaze. His expression remained a mask of impassive stone, but beneath the surface, something darker stirred.</p>
<p>"No," Rex said, his voice deceptively gentle. "He was not."</p>
<p>She didn’t flinch.</p>
<p>"The things I did," she whispered, her eyes burning with a terrible clarity. "In this last month."</p>
<p>"The choices that produced the state he was in when he went to that cliff."</p>
<p>"Were choices that I created the conditions for," Rex countered, stepping into the truth of her accusation without blinking. "Yes."</p>
<p>She held his gaze, her eyes searching his, trying to find the man beneath the weapon. "I know what you are, Rex."</p>
<p>"Not all of it. But enough."</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said, which was simple and undeniable.</p>
<p>"And I am still here," she said. A challenge. A statement of endurance.</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said.</p>
<p>She turned her eyes back to the garden, to the rows of markers that stood like tombstones for a city’s innocence. "Because the children are restored."</p>
<p>"And the city is still standing."</p>
<p>"And Kaeleth is gone... with the temporal disruption that has been addressed."</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said.</p>
<p>"Those are the outcomes," she said, her voice hardening. "Whatever the path was, those are the outcomes."</p>
<p>She looked at the mourners, the broken families, and the hollow-eyed survivors. "I am not going to tell you that the path was acceptable... It was not."</p>
<p>"My son is dead, and the people in this garden are here because of decisions I am partially responsible for and decisions that you are more substantially responsible for."</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said.</p>
<p>"But," she said, and the word carried the crushing weight of her decision, the terrible pragmatism of survival, "the city is still standing."</p>
<p>"Kaeleth is gone. And the children... the children are the correct age."</p>
<p>Rex stared at her, watching the way she compartmentalized agony to maintain order.</p>
<p>"That is sufficient," she said, "for me to continue being where I am."</p>
<p>Rex said nothing. He couldn’t.</p>
<p>Inside, behind the fortress of his controlled expression, Rex was laughing.</p>
<p>It was a cold, silent laughter that tore through his mind like acid. He remembered the taste of Theo’s terror.</p>
<p>He remembered the way the boy’s divine power had felt as he systematically dismantled it, not with a clean strike, but with a calculated, agonizing cruelty.</p>
<p>He remembered the way he had twisted the temporal threads to prolong the boy’s suffering, watching as Theo’s body buckled under the strain of being pulled apart at a cellular level, the wet snap of tendons, and the way the boy’s screams had been frozen in time before being shattered into fragments.</p>
<p>He hadn’t just stopped him; he had broken him, piece by excruciating piece, savoring every moment of the boy’s unraveling.</p>
<p>And now she stood there, praising the ’outcomes,’ thanking him for the restoration, while he stood beside her, playing the part of the loyal protector.</p>
<p>The irony was delicious. She was grateful for the very monster that had fed on her son’s agony.</p>
<p>Elaris appeared at Aurelia’s other shoulder. Her arrival was seamless, the precise timing of someone who had been watching from the periphery, calculating, assessing, waiting for the emotional tide to turn before committing to the space.</p>
<p>Elaris did not speak. She simply looked at Rex, her eyes sharp with unspoken questions, weighing the darkness she sensed clinging to him like oil.</p>
<p>Rex met her gaze. He offered no explanation, no apology.</p>
<p>He simply inclined his head by a fraction of a degree, a micro gesture of recognition between two predators who understood the cost of victory. Elaris acknowledged the signal with a subtle nod before turning her attention to the garden.</p>
<p>The three of them stood at the edge of the ceremony: the grieving mother, the silent observer, and the monster. They occupied the same space, bound by the same tragedy, yet separated by chasms of truth that none would dare cross.</p>
<p>Rex turned away.</p>
