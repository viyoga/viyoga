<p>Rex moved with deliberate purpose. He walked toward the ceremonial dais where Valentina stood, preparing to deliver the formal eulogies.</p>
<p>He didn’t approach the podium; he knew the unspoken rules of power and protocol, but he positioned himself just close enough to be visible, a looming figure of authority standing at the periphery of the light.</p>
<p>He would not claim Valentina’s stage; he would let her perform her duty, but he would ensure that when he spoke, his words carried the weight of the stone itself.</p>
<p>He waited. He watched Valentina weave her words of grief and remembrance, watching the tears stream down the faces of those assembled.</p>
<p>He waited until the formal architecture of the ceremony began to fracture, until the official speeches reached their hollow conclusion, and the ceremony began its slow, painful descent into its final phase.</p>
<p>Then, he spoke.</p>
<p>He didn’t stand on the dais. He didn’t need to.</p>
<p>He stepped to the very edge of the crowd, positioning himself not as a leader delivering an order, but as a participant offering a truth.</p>
<p>His voice didn’t carry through the air alone; he tapped into the Earthen Authority, threading his words through the ambient resonance of the stone beneath their feet.</p>
<p>The vibration traveled through the soles of their shoes, up through their bones, making his words feel as though they were emerging from the earth itself.</p>
<p>He did not speak of Theo. He knew the city’s grief was a poisoned well when it came to the boy, a complicated tangle of love, betrayal, and horror.</p>
<p>To name him would be to reopen wounds that were still weeping blood. And he did not speak of Kaeleth; the people in this garden did not need to hear the name of the monster that had nearly consumed them.</p>
<p>The god’s name was a curse that needed to be buried alongside the dead.</p>
<p>Instead, Rex spoke of Aethelgard.</p>
<p>He spoke of the city not as a collection of buildings, but as a living testament. He spoke of the eight hundred years of history carved into its foundations, eight centuries of people making the conscious, difficult decision that neutral ground was worth the cost.</p>
<p>He spoke of what that accumulation of survival looked like: the scars on the walls, the resilience in the eyes of the survivors, and the stubborn refusal of the city to simply cease existing.</p>
<p>His voice dropped lower, becoming more intimate as he addressed the people in the garden. He didn’t speak to them as ’citizens’ or ’subjects,’ vague, distant categories.</p>
<p>He spoke to them as individuals. He named the blacksmith whose hands had repaired the gates after the last siege; he gestured toward the scholars who maintained the archives; he acknowledged the mothers, the farmers, and the broken soldiers whose quiet contributions formed the invisible scaffolding that kept Aethelgard upright.</p>
<p>He spoke with the brutal honesty of someone who had watched them all, someone who had seen their blood spill and their bones break, and who had counted every loss.</p>
<p>Finally, he spoke about what came next.</p>
<p>It wasn’t a political manifesto. It wasn’t a grand plan for reconstruction or a promise of easy days. It was something far more dangerous: it was a commitment.</p>
<p>He spoke like a man announcing a vow, making his intent public so that the weight of the community would serve as the anvil upon which his resolve was forged.</p>
<p>"This city," Rex’s voice rumbled through the earth, vibrating in the chests of everyone present, "is still here."</p>
<p>"The people in this garden... you are still here."</p>
<p>He paused, letting the resonance fade, letting the silence settle like ash.</p>
<p>"Whatever came at us, and however it came, whether it was divine wrath or human failure, we are standing in this garden."</p>
<p>"The city still breathes... and that is not luck."</p>
<p>"That is the specific, undeniable evidence that this place is worth standing for." He turned his head slowly, his gaze sweeping across the mourners, meeting the eyes of the broken and the weary.</p>
<p>In that moment, he looked less like a man and more like the ancient, unyielding stone he commanded.</p>
<p>"I am choosing," he said, each syllable echoing like a hammer strike, "to stand for it."</p>
<p>He stopped speaking.</p>
<p>The silence that followed Rex’s declaration was not empty; it was heavy, pressurized, like the atmosphere before a catastrophic storm. The crowd remained motionless, suspended in that fragile moment when words cease to be sound and become something tangible.</p>
<p>They were quiet in the way crowds become quiet when they have been handed something too dangerous to speak aloud, a truth that required their absolute stillness to process.</p>
<p>Then, the noise began. It wasn’t the raucous cheering of a victory march, nor was it the keening wail of fresh grief.</p>
<p>It was something more profound and more terrifying: it was the sound of people who had been given something to hold and were gripping it with white-knuckled desperation. It was a low murmur, a collective exhale that sounded like the grinding of tectonic plates—the sound of a society choosing to rebuild on a foundation of scarred stone.</p>
<p>Rex had calculated this reaction precisely; he had orchestrated the emotional architecture of the moment to ensure they didn’t feel comforted but rather fortified.</p>
<p>The morning after the ceremony brought no respite. The sky over the Academy was a bruised purple, the air tasting of ash and residual magic.</p>
<p>Rex sat across from Elizabeth in the Academy’s research chamber, a room lined with ancient texts and humming containment fields. The sterile light of the laboratory made the shadows beneath Rex’s eyes seem deeper, more permanent.</p>
<p>"The construction is complete," Elizabeth said, her fingers dancing across a holographic schematic of the Key.</p>
<p>The device glowed with an unnatural, sickly light. "Or nearly."</p>
<p>"The last phase requires the specific material components that Tremor was documented as possessing."</p>
<p>"Rare earth minerals fused with crystallized essence materials that shouldn’t exist in this reality."</p>
<p>"I will provide them," Rex said.</p>
<p>His voice was flat, devoid of any inflection that might betray the violence required to obtain them.</p>
<p>Elizabeth paused, her quill hovering over a data pad. She looked up, her sharp eyes narrowing as she studied his face.</p>
<p>"The components from Tremor."</p>
<p>"Yes," Rex said.</p>
<p>"Who is—" she began, the question hanging unfinished in the air.</p>
<p>She knew the implications. She knew Tremor was not a merchant or a supplier; he was a force of nature, a being whose very existence was a violation of natural law.</p>
<p>"Not available to provide them personally," Rex cut her off, his tone clipped and final. "The components will be available through the research project’s standard acquisition process."</p>
<p>"Consider it an expedited procurement."</p>
<p>Elizabeth stared at him. She wore the expression she reserved for moments when she received a statement that was technically accurate, surgically precise, and utterly insufficient.</p>
<p>She knew ’expedited procurement’ was a euphemism for something that involved blood, broken bones, and perhaps a corpse. She knew that to get what Rex wanted, something or someone would have to be dismantled.</p>
<p>Inside his mind, Rex could still hear the wet crunch of Tremor’s ribs collapsing under his grip. He could still feel the way the creature’s essence had bled out between his fingers like molten gold mixed with gore.</p>
<p>He had torn the components from the living wreckage of a god, and the memory made the sterile air of the lab feel almost refreshing.</p>
<p>"The Key," Elizabeth said, forcing herself to return to the mathematics, to the safety of the data. "When it is complete..."</p>
<p>"Where does it go?"</p>
<p>"It goes to the meeting," Rex answered. "Which Valentina is scheduling for the day after tomorrow."</p>
<p>"At which we will present the Key as the product of the research project as the miracle of our collective ingenuity and as the primary mechanism for the network’s engagement with the Underlayer."</p>
<p>"And the Underlayer engagement," she pressed, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "The people who go through the Key."</p>
<p>"The volunteers... The soldiers..."</p>
<p>"Will arrive at a prepared site," Rex said, his eyes fixed on the hologram. "In conditions that I have specified."</p>
<p>"The environment will be controlled, the parameters absolute."</p>
<p>The silence stretched between them. Elizabeth looked at the schematic of the Key, a beautiful, terrible thing designed to bridge two worlds, and then she looked at Rex.</p>
<p>She saw the predator beneath the scholar’s robes, the man who would burn a kingdom to save a city.</p>
<p>"How many of them will come back?" she asked quietly.</p>
